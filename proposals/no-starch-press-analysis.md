## 1. Editorial focus (verified from the publisher itself)

From [`nostarch.com/about`](https://nostarch.com/about) and [`nostarch.com/writeforus`](https://nostarch.com/writeforus):

> "We focus on computer programming, security, hacking, alternative operating systems, STEM, and LEGO. Our titles have personality, our authors are passionate, and we read and edit everything we publish."

> "No Starch Press has long had a reputation for publishing unique books on technology, with a focus on open source, security, hacking, programming, alternative operating systems, LEGO, science, and math."

Recurring themes from founder Bill Pollock interviews and their own copy:

- **Hacker and geek culture as the centre of gravity.** Their flagships include *Hacking: The Art of Exploitation*, *Practical Packet Analysis*, *Android Security Internals*, *Absolute FreeBSD/OpenBSD*, the *Manga Guide* series, *Python Crash Course*, and *Automate the Boring Stuff with Python*. Topic and tone matter equally.
- **Demanding but enjoyable.** Their jobs page calls out "obsession with quality and the belief that technical books should be both demanding and enjoyable."
- **Open source and alternative OSes are house blends.** Linux, BSD, lesser-known languages, Lego, retrocomputing.
- **They edit everything heavily.** A real differentiator vs Packt or self-published. Implication: authors must be willing to be edited.
- **They publish topics they personally care about.** Pollock: *"I publish books on topics that I care about. I like to support passionate communities and passionate people."*

## 3. What No Starch wants in a proposal — verified requirements

From [`nostarch.com/writeforus`](https://nostarch.com/writeforus) and the authorspublish.com summary that paraphrases it:

### 3.1 What you must include

1. **A clear description of the book** — what it's about and what makes it different. They explicitly say this is one of the *two most important factors*, alongside the TOC.
2. **A detailed outline** — at minimum, chapter titles plus first-level headings within each chapter.
3. **Audience definition** — who is the target reader and how does the book meet their needs?
4. **Writing samples**, if available — sample chapter or other published technical writing in your voice.
5. **Author bio** — credentials, professional experience, online presence.

### 3.2 What they care about beyond the form

- **Topic must fit their list.** Programming, security, hacking, open source, alternative OSes, STEM, math, LEGO. Web3 *can* fit (security/hacker angle); pure finance won't.
- **Personality matters.** They explicitly say "Our titles have personality, our authors are passionate." Sterile, vendor-flavoured manuscripts get rejected.
- **They edit everything.** Authors must be willing to be edited heavily and revise.
- **They publish for "passionate communities."** A book that names a specific tribe (smart-contract auditors, validator operators, AI red-teamers) reads as more aligned than a generic survey.
- **They favour visually rich formats.** Diagrams, code listings, full-colour print, the *Manga Guide* series, comics. A pitch with strong visual treatment lands better than wall-of-text.
- **They prize hands-on / project-based pedagogy.** Practical Deep Learning, Cracking Codes with Python, Machine Learning for Kids, Bitcoin for the Befuddled — all hands-on. A "build N things" structure fits their template.

### 3.3 What they implicitly avoid

- **Pure-business, pure-finance, or pure-investor books.** They've dabbled (Bitcoin for the Befuddled is borderline) but the consistent pattern is *engineering* angle.
- **"Blockchain for [non-tech industry]" survey books.** None on the list. Don't pitch one.
- **Vendor-flavoured manuals** dressed as books.
- **Pure-academic monographs.** That's Springer / Cambridge UP / MIT Press territory.
- **Topics they've already saturated.** No new "intro to Python" or "intro to LLMs" will be commissioned.

## 4. The forthcoming pipeline as a signal

The 2026 Early Access list is the clearest signal of editorial direction:

- **AI security and AI red-teaming are a focus area.** *Practical AI Security* (April 2026) + *Red Teaming AI* (June 2026, 500 pp) is a one-two punch.
- **Post-training, fine-tuning, RAG, and production AI** — *Post-Training* (Fall 2026) and *Practical Deep Learning 2e* (2025).
- **Cybersecurity foundations refresh** — *Foundations of Cybersecurity 2e* (March 2026).
- **Social-platform security** — *Social Media Exposed* (May 2026).
- **Systems / OS internals** — *The Linux Memory Manager* in pre-order.

**They are not announcing any blockchain, Web3, or quantum titles for 2026.**

## 5. Strategic implications — what to pitch them

### 5.1 Highest-fit pitches (matches No Starch voice + fills a gap)

| Pitch | Why it fits |
|---|---|
| **The Manga Guide to Zero-Knowledge Proofs** | Manga Guide is a No Starch franchise; they did *The Manga Guide to Cryptography* (2018). ZK is famously hard to explain. Format + topic + house style align. |
| **Smart Contract Auditor's Field Manual** | Hacker-culture aligned; security-tilted; fills a gap in their otherwise empty blockchain shelf. Positioning: *Hacking: The Art of Exploitation* but for EVM. |
| **Postmortems: 50 Smart Contract Exploits** | Hacker culture, security, hands-on, narrative. Matches the *Practical Packet Analysis* / *Malware Data Science* template. |
| **Cracking Smart Contracts** | Direct extension of Sweigart's *Cracking Codes with Python* template into the EVM. Same author archetype, beginner-friendly, hands-on. |
| **Practical Post-Quantum Cryptography** | Aumasson already wrote *Serious Cryptography 2e* and *Crypto Dictionary*. A book-length PQC follow-up is the natural sequel. Engineering migration playbook. |
| **Quantum Computing for Hackers** | Plays to their hacker-and-builder voice. Grover/Shor as offensive primitives, quantum random-number generation, side-channel implications, defensive PQC. |
| **Red Teaming the Blockchain** | Symmetrical to *Red Teaming AI* (which they've already commissioned). Smart-contract red-team field manual. |

### 5.2 Pitches that won't land at No Starch (better elsewhere)

| Pitch | Why it doesn't fit |
|---|---|
| Tokenomics: A Mathematical Treatment | Too finance-y. Better at Wiley / Springer / academic. |
| RWA Tokenization Handbook | Too regulatory/finance. Better at Wiley or O'Reilly. |
| CBDC Engineering | Too institutional. Springer / Cambridge UP. |
| Quantum for Product Managers | Too business-tilted. Better as an O'Reilly Radar exec briefing or self-published. |
| Account Abstraction in Practice | Better at O'Reilly or Manning, where the modern Web3 stack already lives. |
| Modular Blockchain Architecture | Same — O'Reilly / Manning. |

### 5.3 The ideal first pitch

If you have one shot at No Starch and want to define their blockchain list, the strongest pitch is:

> **"Cracking Smart Contracts: Build, Break, and Defend Decentralised Applications"**
>
> Hands-on, project-based, security-tilted handbook. Reader builds a working DeFi protocol over the course of the book and then breaks it (re-entrancy, oracle manipulation, governance attack, MEV sandwich, signature replay), then audits and hardens it. Covers Foundry, Slither, Echidna, Halmos, modern Solidity 0.8.x patterns, and post-Pectra account-abstraction-aware vulnerabilities.
>
> Voice: Sweigart-meets-Antonopoulos. Author archetype: practitioner auditor (Trail of Bits / OpenZeppelin / Spearbit / Cyfrin) + co-author with educator background.
>
> This pitch hits *every* No Starch heuristic: hacker culture, security, hands-on, project-based, fills the blockchain gap, doesn't compete with anything in their catalog or 2026 pipeline.
