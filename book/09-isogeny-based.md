# Chapter 9: Isogeny-Based Cryptography

## 9.1 Elliptic Curves and Isogenies

### Elliptic Curve Recap

An elliptic curve E over a field K is a smooth projective curve of genus 1 with a designated rational point. In short Weierstrass form:

```
E: y² = x³ + ax + b  (where 4a³ + 27b² ≠ 0)
```

Key properties:
- Points on E form an abelian group under a geometric addition law
- Over F_p: |E(F_p)| ≈ p (Hasse's theorem: |p + 1 - |E(F_p)|| ≤ 2√p)
- The **j-invariant** j(E) = 1728 · (4a³)/(4a³ + 27b²) classifies isomorphism classes

### What Is an Isogeny?

An **isogeny** φ: E₁ → E₂ is a surjective group homomorphism between elliptic curves that is also a morphism of algebraic varieties (a rational map). Informally, it's a "structure-preserving map" between curves.

Key properties of isogenies:
- **Degree:** deg(φ) equals the size of the kernel: |ker(φ)| = deg(φ) for separable isogenies
- **Dual isogeny:** Every isogeny φ: E₁ → E₂ has a dual φ̂: E₂ → E₁ with φ̂ ∘ φ = [deg(φ)]
- **Composition:** Isogenies can be composed; deg(φ ∘ ψ) = deg(φ) · deg(ψ)
- **Vélu's formulas:** Given a subgroup G ⊆ E, compute the isogeny φ: E → E/G explicitly

### The Isogeny Graph

Fix a prime p and consider all elliptic curves over F_p (up to isomorphism). The **ℓ-isogeny graph** has:
- Vertices: j-invariants of elliptic curves
- Edges: ℓ-isogenies between curves

For supersingular curves, this graph has remarkable properties:
- **Regular:** Every vertex has exactly ℓ + 1 outgoing edges (for prime ℓ)
- **Ramanujan:** Optimal spectral gap, excellent expansion properties
- **Connected:** For supersingular curves over F_{p²}

## 9.2 Supersingular vs. Ordinary Curves

### Ordinary Curves

- Endomorphism ring: An order in an imaginary quadratic field
- Isogeny graph: "Volcanic" structure with well-defined levels
- Used in CSIDH (Commutative Supersingular Isogeny Diffie-Hellman)

### Supersingular Curves

- Endomorphism ring: A maximal order in a quaternion algebra
- Isogeny graph: Ramanujan graph (excellent mixing properties)
- Used in SIKE/SIDH (now broken) and SQISign
- Number of supersingular curves over F_{p²}: ≈ p/12

The choice between ordinary and supersingular curves affects both the available hard problems and the efficiency of protocols.

## 9.3 Hard Problems in Isogeny-Based Cryptography

### Supersingular Isogeny Problem

**Definition:** Given two supersingular elliptic curves E₁, E₂ over F_{p²}, find an isogeny φ: E₁ → E₂.

**Complexity:**
- Best classical algorithm: O(p^(1/4)) using meet-in-the-middle on isogeny graphs
- Best quantum algorithm: O(p^(1/6)) using quantum walk on isogeny graphs (Biasse-Jao-Sankar)
- No polynomial-time quantum algorithm known (when no auxiliary information is provided)

### Endomorphism Ring Problem

**Definition:** Given a supersingular curve E over F_{p²}, compute End(E) (its full endomorphism ring).

**Significance:**
- Computing the endomorphism ring is equivalent to computing isogenies (Eisenträger et al.)
- Provides a theoretical foundation for the hardness of isogeny problems
- The "Deuring correspondence" connects endomorphism rings to quaternion algebra ideals

### CSIDH Problem

**Definition:** Given ordinary curves E₁ and E₂ connected by a class group action, find the group element [a] such that [a] · E₁ = E₂.

**Complexity:**
- Equivalent to computing class group relations
- Best quantum algorithm: Subexponential using Kuperberg's algorithm for hidden shift
- Concrete quantum security depends on parameters and Kuperberg's algorithm variant

## 9.4 SIDH/SIKE: Rise and Fall

### The SIDH Protocol (Jao-De Feo, 2011)

SIDH (Supersingular Isogeny Diffie-Hellman) was the first practical isogeny-based key exchange:

**Setup:**
- Supersingular curve E₀ over F_{p²} where p = 2^a · 3^b · f - 1
- Public points (P_A, Q_A) generating E₀[2^a] and (P_B, Q_B) generating E₀[3^b]

**Key Exchange:**
- Alice: Secret isogeny φ_A: E₀ → E_A of degree 2^a (kernel generated by P_A + [m_A]Q_A)
- Bob: Secret isogeny φ_B: E₀ → E_B of degree 3^b (kernel generated by P_B + [m_B]Q_B)
- Alice publishes: (E_A, φ_A(P_B), φ_A(Q_B))
- Bob publishes: (E_B, φ_B(P_A), φ_B(Q_A))
- Shared secret: j(E₀/(⟨P_A + [m_A]Q_A, P_B + [m_B]Q_B⟩))

**Key advantage:** Extremely small key sizes (~330 bytes for 128-bit security)

### SIKE (Supersingular Isogeny Key Encapsulation)

SIKE was the KEM version of SIDH, submitted to NIST's PQC competition:
- Smallest key sizes of any PQC candidate
- Public key: 330 bytes (Level 1)
- Ciphertext: 346 bytes
- But: Slow (milliseconds per operation)

### The Devastating Attack (2022)

In July 2022, Castryck and Decru published a polynomial-time attack on SIDH/SIKE:

**Key insight:** The auxiliary points (φ_A(P_B), φ_A(Q_B)) published alongside E_A reveal too much information. Combined with the Kani lemma connecting isogenies of abelian surfaces, this allows efficient recovery of the secret isogeny.

**Impact:**
- SIKE broken at all security levels in minutes
- Fundamental structural flaw, not a parameter issue
- The attack exploits the specific auxiliary information SIDH requires

**Lessons:**
- Publishing torsion point images is dangerous
- The algebraic structure of abelian varieties provides unexpected attack vectors
- Relatively young mathematical constructions carry higher cryptanalytic risk

## 9.5 CSIDH: Commutative Group Actions

### Construction

CSIDH (Commutative Supersingular Isogeny Diffie-Hellman) uses a different approach:

**Setting:**
- Ordinary-looking supersingular curves over F_p (curves with F_p-rational structure)
- The ideal class group Cl(O) acts on these curves via isogenies
- This action is commutative: [a]([b]·E) = [b]([a]·E)

**Key Exchange:**
- Public: Starting curve E₀ over F_p
- Alice: Secret class group element [a], publishes E_A = [a]·E₀
- Bob: Secret class group element [b], publishes E_B = [b]·E₀
- Shared: [a]·E_B = [a]·[b]·E₀ = [b]·[a]·E₀ = [b]·E_A

### Security of CSIDH

CSIDH is NOT broken by the Castryck-Decru attack (no auxiliary points published).

However, CSIDH faces a different quantum threat: **Kuperberg's algorithm** for the hidden shift problem provides a subexponential quantum attack.

Concrete security estimates depend on:
- Which variant of Kuperberg's algorithm is used
- Quantum memory assumptions
- Whether the attacker uses classical preprocessing

Current estimates suggest CSIDH-512 provides approximately 62-128 bits of quantum security (highly debated).

### CSIDH Variants

- **CSIDH-512:** Original parameters, security debated
- **CSIDH-1024:** Higher security but slower
- **CTIDH:** Constant-time implementation of CSIDH
- **SCALLOP/CSURF:** Variants with different efficiency trade-offs

## 9.6 SQISign: Isogeny-Based Signatures

### Overview

SQISign (Short Quaternion and Isogeny Signature) is the most promising isogeny-based signature scheme:

**Key Idea:** Use the Deuring correspondence between:
- Supersingular isogenies ↔ Ideals in quaternion algebras

**Properties:**
- Very compact: ~177-byte signatures (smallest of any PQC signature)
- Public key: 64 bytes
- Slow signing (~seconds on commodity hardware)
- Moderate verification (~100 ms)

### Construction

1. **Key generation:** Choose a random supersingular curve E_A; the secret is a representation of the isogeny from E₀ to E_A as a quaternion ideal
2. **Signing:** Generate a commitment isogeny, compute challenge via hash, solve the challenge using quaternion algebra
3. **Verification:** Check that the response isogeny has the correct degree and endpoints

### Security

SQISign's security is based on:
- The endomorphism ring problem for supersingular curves
- No known polynomial-time quantum algorithm
- Not affected by the SIDH attack (no torsion point images)
- Under active study — newer than lattice/code-based schemes

### Current Status

- Under consideration for NIST additional signatures
- Performance improvements ongoing (SQISign2D, AprèsSQI)
- Concerns about implementation complexity and side-channel resistance
- Still an active research area

## 9.7 Other Isogeny-Based Constructions

### FESTA (Fast Encryption from Supersingular Torsion Attacks)

- Encryption scheme using the structure from the SIDH attack constructively
- Preliminary — not yet mature enough for standardization

### SCALLOP (Scalable Group Action)

- Based on group actions from oriented supersingular curves
- Aims for efficient group-action-based protocols
- Avoids Kuperberg's attack through careful design

### Threshold and Advanced Protocols

Isogeny-based group actions enable:
- Non-interactive key exchange
- Threshold protocols
- Oblivious transfer
- Verifiable random functions

## 9.9 The State of Isogeny-Based Cryptography (2025-2026)

### What's Broken
- SIDH/SIKE: Completely broken (polynomial-time attack)
- Some CSIDH parameter sets: Questionable quantum security

### What Survives
- CSIDH with large parameters: Potentially secure but debated
- SQISign: No known efficient attack
- Group-action-based constructions (no torsion point leakage)

### Active Research Directions
- Improving SQISign efficiency (parallelization, better algorithms)
- New constructions avoiding known attack patterns
- Understanding the boundary between breakable and secure isogeny problems
- Formal security models for group-action cryptography

### Practical Outlook

Isogeny-based cryptography is currently NOT recommended for production deployment:
- Too young as a field (major surprises still occurring)
- Performance limitations for most applications
- Limited implementation experience
- No NIST standardized algorithm from this family

However, isogeny-based schemes may find niches where compact sizes are paramount and performance is acceptable. SQISign remains under NIST evaluation for additional signature standards.

## 9.10 Key Takeaways

- Isogeny-based cryptography uses maps between elliptic curves as its foundation
- SIDH/SIKE was broken in 2022 — a cautionary tale about auxiliary information leakage
- CSIDH provides a commutative group action but faces subexponential quantum attacks
- SQISign offers the smallest signatures of any PQC scheme but is slow
- The field is mathematically rich but lacks the cryptanalytic maturity of lattices or codes
- No isogeny-based algorithm has been standardized by NIST
- Current recommendation: Do not deploy isogeny-based schemes in production; monitor research progress
